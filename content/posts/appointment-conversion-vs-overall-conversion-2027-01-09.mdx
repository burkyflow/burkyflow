"Our leads convert at eight percent." That sentence is true at most dealerships and useless at all of them, because it does not say where the other 92 percent went. Did they never get a response? Did they talk to someone and not book? Did they book and not show? Did they show and not buy? Each of those is a different problem with a different fix. Overall conversion blends them into one number nobody can act on.

## The funnel, in four steps

Split lead-to-sale into the steps the customer actually takes:

1. **Contact rate.** Share of leads that had a real two-way exchange (not an auto-reply).
2. **Appointment rate.** Share of contacted leads that booked a visit or a call.
3. **Show rate.** Share of appointments that happened.
4. **Close rate.** Share of shows that bought.

Multiply them and you get overall conversion. Look at them separately and you see the leak.

## What each step says

**Low contact rate** means the response is too slow or the channel is wrong. This is the 5-minute rule problem: leads arriving at night and weekends with nobody answering. Fix: automation that responds instantly and books, with humans alerted.

**Low appointment rate** with a good contact rate means the conversation is not asking for the visit, or is asking badly. Fix: scripts that offer two slots instead of asking "when works for you," and reply handling that moves "no, I haven't" to a person fast.

**Low show rate** means appointments are set loosely or not confirmed. Fix: confirmation at booking, a reminder the day before with a one-tap reschedule, and a call from a person for high-value appointments.

**Low close rate** is a sales floor or pricing issue, not a BDC issue. It is the only step where more leads would not help.

## Why appointment rate is the BDC's number

The BDC controls the first two steps and influences the third. Its performance is appointment rate and show rate, measured on contacted leads. Judging a BDC on overall conversion punishes it for the sales floor's close rate and rewards it for nothing it did. Judging it on appointment rate alone, without show rate, rewards setting weak appointments. Measure both.

## What automation changes

Good automation moves the first three numbers:

- **Contact rate** rises because every lead gets a real response within a minute, at any hour. An [AI receptionist](/services/ai-voice-receptionist) can call back, text, and book without waiting for a person.
- **Appointment rate** rises because the conversation offers specific times and routes hot replies to a human.
- **Show rate** rises because confirmations and reminders are automatic and rescheduling is one reply away.

Close rate should not change, and if it falls, the automation is setting appointments with the wrong customers.

## Counting appointments honestly

Automation makes it easy to inflate the appointment count. Rules that keep it honest:

- An appointment is a specific date and time the customer agreed to, not a "sometime this week."
- Appointments set by the system and by people are counted separately.
- Rescheduled appointments count once.
- No-shows are counted as no-shows, not rebooked silently.
- Show rate is measured against appointments set, not against appointments confirmed.

A [CRM](/services/crm-lead-systems) that enforces these definitions produces a dashboard the dealer principal can trust, which is the subject of a later post in this series.

## What to report weekly

- Leads by source
- Contact rate, with the share contacted in under five minutes
- Appointment rate on contacted leads
- Show rate
- Close rate on shows
- Overall conversion, as a product of the four, for reference

Put the four steps side by side with last week's numbers and the leak is obvious. Fix the step, not the total.
