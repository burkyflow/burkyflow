Dealerships buy automation for lead generation, chat, digital retailing, inventory pricing, and reputation. Each one competes for the same new customers against every other store running the same tools. Post-sale F&I recovery competes with nobody, because the customers are already yours and the products are already in your menu. Of everything a dealership can automate, it is the highest-margin option, and the least often bought.

## Why the margin is high

Three reasons, each structural:

1. **No acquisition cost.** The customer bought a car from you. Their contact details, vehicle, and loan are on file. The cost of reaching them is the cost of a text.
2. **High-gross products.** Service contracts, GAP, maintenance plans, and protection products carry gross margins that most other dealership revenue cannot match.
3. **Known need.** The triggers are facts: coverage ending, loan-to-value exposure, a repair that just happened. The message is relevant by construction, so response rates are a multiple of cold marketing.

Put together, the cost per product sold through recovery is a fraction of the cost per product sold through any new-customer channel.

## The math, generically

Take a cohort of 100 deliveries. Suppose a share of customers declined a service contract at delivery, and a further share will see factory coverage end within two years. A recovery program contacts those customers at the right moment with a specific offer. Even a modest enrollment rate on the contacted group, multiplied by the product gross, produces a number that compares well with the F&I gross earned at delivery, for a program cost that is mostly fixed.

Run the same calculation for GAP on exposed loans, maintenance plans at the first paid service, and tire and wheel at the first rotation, and the lines stack. The store does not need every product to recover well; it needs the engine to run every trigger so that each product recovers something.

Plug your own numbers in: declines recorded, trigger counts per month, your product gross, and a conservative enrollment rate. The result is the budget for the program.

## What it requires

The margin is only available if the inputs exist:

- **Declines recorded at delivery.** The F&I menu must log which products were offered and declined. Without this, the program starts blind.
- **DMS access.** Warranty dates, loan terms, and repair orders, at least nightly.
- **A compliant dispatch layer.** Correct product terminology, consent, opt-out, and a contact budget.
- **A finance desk that answers replies.** Within minutes, with the deal on screen. An [AI receptionist](/services/ai-voice-receptionist) covers the hours the desk cannot.
- **Honest reporting.** Recovery gross counted separately from delivery gross, reconciled to the DMS.

## Compared with the alternatives

- **Lead generation** buys strangers at rising prices, shared with competitors.
- **Chat and digital retailing** improve conversion of visitors you already paid to attract.
- **Inventory tools** improve margin on vehicles, which is thinner than product margin.
- **Reputation tools** protect the brand but do not produce revenue directly.

All useful. None has recovery's combination of zero acquisition cost and high product gross.

## Why it is rarely bought

- Nobody owns it. F&I is paid on today's deals; the BDC is measured on new leads.
- Declines are not recorded, so the opportunity is invisible.
- The DMS integration is assumed to be hard.
- Vendors sell what is easy to demo, and a two-year lifecycle trigger does not demo well.

A dealer who looks past the demo and asks for the cohort math usually finds the largest unworked number in the store.

## What to measure

- Declined products logged per delivery
- Triggers fired, contacts, replies, enrollments by product
- Recovery gross per 100 deliveries
- Program cost, and net margin on recovery
- Cancellation rate within 60 days
