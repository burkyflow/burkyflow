Every automated follow-up starts with a question: what event fires it? The answer is usually chosen by whoever built the workflow, based on what was easy to detect. It should be chosen based on what the customer is actually deciding at that moment. The same message, fired on the first yes, the second yes, or the no, produces three different results.

## The three moments

Take a customer who called about a repair, got an estimate, and is deciding:

- **The first yes.** The customer agreed to the diagnosis. They are engaged but have not committed money.
- **The second yes.** The customer approved the repair, or booked the appointment. Commitment made.
- **The no.** The customer declined, went quiet, or said "not now."

Each moment calls for a different automated response, and firing the wrong one is how automation gets a bad name.

## What to fire on the first yes

The first yes is interest. The right automation makes the next step easy:

- Send the estimate in writing, with the financed monthly figure beside the total.
- Offer two appointment slots, not a link to a calendar.
- Set a short timer: if no second yes within 48 hours, treat it as a soft no and move to the no path.

What not to fire: a review request, a product offer, or a survey. Nothing has been delivered yet.

## What to fire on the second yes

The second yes is commitment. Automation here should protect the commitment:

- Confirmation with the date, the amount, and what to bring.
- A reminder the day before, with a reschedule option that works by reply.
- After completion: the thank-you, the review request, and the next lifecycle event (the next service due date goes into the engine).

What not to fire: more selling. The customer just said yes; let it land.

## What to fire on the no

The no is where most automation either stops or becomes a nuisance. The right response depends on which no it was:

1. **"Not now."** Set a re-check date based on the item (a week for brakes, a month for a timing belt) and send one message then. Not a sequence; one message.
2. **Silence.** One follow-up at 48 hours that reframes the question as a choice between two next steps. Then a re-check at the natural interval.
3. **"Went elsewhere."** Close the opportunity. Keep the lifecycle triggers (service due, warranty end) running; those are about the car, not the lost job.
4. **"Stop."** Opt out everywhere, immediately.

The declined-repair, declined-F&I, and negative-equity funnels in this series all live on the no path. They work because they fire once, at a specific moment, with a specific reason.

## Rules that keep timing honest

- **One trigger, one message.** If a message needs three reasons, it is three triggers.
- **A contact budget.** No customer hears from the business more than a set number of times per month across all funnels.
- **A quiet period after any yes.** Suppress offers for a fixed window after a booking or purchase.
- **Re-check, not repeat.** A no gets a dated re-check, never a drip.
- **Humans on the hot replies.** Any reply that signals "not yet" or a question goes to a person within minutes.

## Building it

In a [workflow platform](/services/workflow-automation), each moment is a separate trigger with its own path: a webhook or a record-state change for the first yes, an appointment-created event for the second yes, and a timer or a classified reply for the no. A [CRM](/services/crm-lead-systems) holds the opportunity state so the paths do not fire over each other. An [AI receptionist](/services/ai-voice-receptionist) can handle the conversation that follows any of them, because booking and answering questions are the same in every path.

## What to measure

- Conversion from first yes to second yes, and time between them
- Share of no responses that convert on the re-check
- Complaints and opt-outs by trigger
- Contact load per customer per month

Opt-outs by trigger is the early warning. A trigger that produces opt-outs is firing at the wrong moment.
