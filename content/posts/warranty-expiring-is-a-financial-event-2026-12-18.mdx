The day a factory warranty expires, the customer's relationship with the car changes. Repairs that were free now cost money. The customer starts thinking about a service contract, about whether to keep the car, about where to get it serviced now that the dealer visit is no longer required. Nothing visibly happens that day, but financially it is one of the most important dates in the ownership cycle. It is also sitting in your DMS, unused.

## Ownership as a series of dates

Every vehicle you sold or service has a timeline you can predict at delivery:

- **First service interval.** Oil, filters, tire rotation. The first chance to make the service department the customer's default.
- **Each scheduled maintenance.** Known intervals by model and mileage.
- **Factory warranty end.** Bumper-to-bumper, then powertrain, on known dates or mileages.
- **Loan or lease milestones.** 12 months (refinance review), equity crossover, lease maturity.
- **Age-based events.** Tires at a known mileage, batteries at a known age, major services at set intervals.
- **Replacement window.** When repair costs and payment position make a new vehicle sensible.

A dealership that works these dates is never cold-calling. Every message is about something that is actually happening to the customer's car or loan.

## The warranty-end trigger in detail

The warranty end is the richest single trigger because it opens three conversations at once:

1. **Service contract.** "Your factory coverage ends on [date]. Here are vehicle service contract options from the store that services your car." Correct terminology, specific date, clear next step.
2. **Pre-expiration inspection.** "Before your coverage ends, bring the car in so we can check for anything still covered." A real service, appreciated by customers, and a visit that lands the customer in the lane.
3. **Replacement.** For customers whose equity and payment position fit, the warranty end is a natural moment to show a newer vehicle at a similar payment, with fresh coverage.

Send them in that order, spaced over the 90 days before expiry, not all at once.

## Building the lifecycle engine

The engine is simple once the data is connected:

**Capture the dates at delivery.** In-service date, warranty terms, loan terms, and first service due go into the CRM as fields, not as notes.

**Define the triggers.** Each date minus a lead time: warranty end minus 90 days, service due minus 14 days, loan anniversary on the day.

**Write one message per trigger.** Short, specific, with a single next step.

**Route replies to the right desk.** Service replies go to the advisors or an [AI receptionist](/services/ai-voice-receptionist) that can book against the shop schedule. F&I replies go to the finance office. Sales replies go to the BDC.

**Suppress sensibly.** A customer who just had a service visit does not need a service reminder. A customer who bought a service contract does not need the offer again.

A [workflow platform](/services/workflow-automation) connected to the DMS and CRM runs all of this without anyone keeping a calendar.

## Why this beats campaigns

A quarterly "service special" email goes to everyone, about nothing in particular, and gets ignored. A lifecycle message goes to one customer, about their car, on the day it matters. Response rates are not comparable. Lifecycle marketing also compounds: every vehicle delivered adds a decade of dated events to the engine.

## What to measure

- Customers reached per trigger per month
- Service appointments booked from service triggers
- Service contracts sold from warranty-end triggers
- Vehicles sold from replacement-window triggers
- Service retention: share of sold vehicles still serviced with you at 12, 24, and 36 months

Retention is the long-term score. Lifecycle triggers are how you move it.

## Compliance

Each message needs consent for the channel used and an opt-out. Product messages must use accurate terms: service contract, not warranty. Keep records of which trigger produced which message, so every contact can be explained.
