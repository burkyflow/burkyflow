Businesses that start automating usually build one funnel at a time: a reactivation campaign here, a service reminder there, a lease-end sequence later. Each one is wired separately, lives in a different tool or a different corner of the same tool, and is maintained by whoever built it. Within a year there are nine funnels and nobody is sure which ones still work.

The alternative is one engine. Every funnel is the same machine with a different trigger, a different message, and a different person handling the reply. Build the machine once, and adding a funnel becomes a configuration change rather than a project.

## What every funnel has in common

Look at any revenue-recovery funnel and it breaks into the same five parts:

1. **A trigger.** An event or a data condition: warranty ending, equity crossing zero, repair declined, 18 months since last service.
2. **A segment rule.** Who qualifies and who is excluded: consent, recent contact, already bought the product.
3. **A message.** One text or email, specific to the trigger, with a single next step.
4. **A reply handler.** What happens when the customer answers: book, route to a person, opt out, or mark not now and set a re-check date.
5. **A scorecard.** Contacted, replied, booked, sold, revenue.

Refinance, GAP re-marketing, declined repairs, and HVAC tune-ups all fit this shape. The difference is the content of each part, not the structure.

## Building the engine

**Data layer.** One place where the customer, the vehicle or equipment, the loan or job history, and the consent record are joined. For a dealership that means the DMS, CRM, and loan system feeding one customer record. For a home services company it means the field service software and the CRM. A [CRM and lead system](/services/crm-lead-systems) built for this is the foundation.

**Trigger evaluation.** A scheduled job that checks every record against every active trigger rule and creates an "opportunity" for each match. Running nightly is enough for most triggers.

**Message dispatch.** A single sending path that applies the consent check, the suppression rules, and the opt-out footer to every message, regardless of which funnel produced it.

**Reply routing.** One inbox logic: classify the reply, book if possible, otherwise route to the right desk with the context attached. An [AI receptionist](/services/ai-voice-receptionist) can handle the booking conversation for any funnel, because the booking step is the same.

**Reporting.** One dashboard, with funnel as a filter rather than a separate report.

## Adding a funnel

Once the engine exists, a new funnel is a short checklist:

- Define the trigger rule (which fields, which thresholds).
- Write the message and the reply options.
- Name the desk that handles replies.
- Set the re-check interval for "not now."
- Turn it on and watch the scorecard.

A day, not a month. More importantly, the compliance controls, the opt-out handling, and the reporting are inherited, not rebuilt.

## Why separate funnels fail

Separately built funnels drift. One uses the old opt-out language. One sends to customers who already bought. Two fire on the same customer in the same week. Nobody can say what the total contact load on a customer is. One engine fixes all of that by design: a customer is one record, with one contact history and one set of rules.

## What to measure, engine-wide

- Active funnels and triggers evaluated per night
- Messages sent per customer per month (contact load)
- Reply rate by funnel
- Revenue by funnel, on the same scale
- Time to add the last funnel

The last one is the test of the architecture. If adding a funnel still takes a project, it is not an engine yet.
