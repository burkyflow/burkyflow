A customer fills in a form on a Saturday evening: interested in a used truck, wants to know if it is still available. By the time the BDC opens on Monday, the customer has heard from three other dealers, visited one, and possibly bought. The lead was good. The response was slow. That pattern repeats at every dealership that measures leads by the week instead of by the minute.

The 5-minute rule is simple: every new lead reaches a human, or a system that can act like one, within five minutes, at any hour.

## Why five minutes

Lead interest decays fast. The customer who submitted a form is still looking at the vehicle, still on the website, still in the mood to talk. Five minutes later they are checking a competitor's listing. An hour later they are doing something else and your call is an interruption. A day later your message is one of several and you are competing on price alone.

Fast response is not about pressure. It is about catching the customer while the question is still open in their mind.

## What breaks the rule

Most dealerships do not fail the 5-minute rule because people are lazy. They fail because of structure:

- **Hours.** Leads arrive 24 hours a day. People work 10.
- **Routing.** The lead goes to a shared inbox, a CRM queue, or a salesperson who is with a customer.
- **Lunch and shift changes.** Nobody owns the queue for 40 minutes.
- **Lead source lag.** Some third-party sources deliver leads in batches, minutes or hours late.
- **The wrong first touch.** An auto-reply email that says "thanks, we'll be in touch" is not a response. It is a receipt.

## Building a response that holds

The rule holds only if the first response does not depend on who is available:

**Instant acknowledgment that does something.** Within a minute of the lead arriving, the customer gets a text that answers the question or asks the next one: "Yes, the 2022 Tacoma is available. Want to come see it today or tomorrow?" Not a thank-you note.

**An actual conversation.** If the customer replies, the system keeps going: confirms the time, captures the trade, answers basic questions. An [AI receptionist](/services/ai-voice-receptionist) can also call the customer back within a minute, confirm interest, and book the visit, sounding like a helpful person rather than a recording.

**A human, alerted with context.** The lead, the vehicle, and the conversation so far go to a salesperson or BDC agent as a priority task. During business hours, the person joins the conversation within minutes. After hours, the appointment is already booked and the person confirms it in the morning.

**Escalation when nobody answers.** If the assigned person does not touch the lead in five minutes, it goes to the next person, then to a manager. The customer never knows; they just get a response.

A [CRM built for this](/services/crm-lead-systems) handles the routing and the escalation. The people handle the selling.

## Measuring it honestly

Response time is easy to game and easy to misreport. Measure what the customer experiences:

- **Time to first real response.** The first message that answers or asks something, not the auto-receipt.
- **Time to human contact.** When a person first spoke to, texted, or called the customer.
- **Share of leads under five minutes**, by hour of day and day of week.
- **Appointment rate and show rate**, split by response time buckets.

The last split usually ends the argument. Leads answered in under five minutes set appointments at a rate that slower leads never match.

## Where the weekend leads go

Run the report for Saturday after 5 p.m. and Sunday. If the 5-minute share is near zero, that is where the dealership is losing deals, and it is the easiest place to fix, because the fix is automation and the weekend leads are already arriving.

## Compliance

A text response requires consent, which the lead form should capture, and every message needs an opt-out. Calls outside permitted hours are a problem; the system should hold the callback until the allowed window and text in the meantime.
