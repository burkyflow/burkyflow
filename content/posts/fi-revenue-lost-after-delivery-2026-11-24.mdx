Every delivery closes two sales. The vehicle sale is the one everyone watches. The second is the F&I sale, and for most customers it ends the moment they sign and drive off. The products they declined, the coverage that will expire, and the loan that will become refinance-ready in a year all leave the building with them.

That is F&I revenue walking out the door every day. It is not lost because the customer said no. It is lost because nobody asks again at the right moment.

## Where F&I revenue goes after delivery

Think about what happens to a typical deal in the months after it funds:

- **Declined products.** The customer passed on the vehicle service contract, GAP, tire and wheel, or prepaid maintenance. In the finance office that "no" was final. Six months later the same customer may feel differently, especially after a first repair bill.
- **Expiring coverage.** The factory warranty or an existing service contract runs out on a known date. That date is a financial event for the customer, and it sits in your DMS.
- **Rate and equity changes.** A customer who financed at a high rate builds equity as they pay down the loan. At some point a refinance saves them real money. If you do not offer it, their bank or a credit union will.
- **Lease maturity.** Lease customers have a fixed decision window. Most dealerships work it late, if at all.

None of these are new leads. They are existing customers with a known vehicle, a known loan, and a known timeline. That makes them the cheapest revenue a dealership can pursue.

## Why F&I follow-up rarely happens

The reasons are practical, not strategic:

1. **The data is split.** The sale lives in the DMS, the customer in the CRM, the service history in the service system, and the F&I menu in a fourth tool. Nobody sees a single customer timeline.
2. **Nobody owns the second ask.** The F&I manager is paid on deals in the office today. The BDC is measured on fresh leads and appointments. Post-sale product follow-up falls between them.
3. **The timing is specific.** A declined service contract is worth re-offering just before the factory warranty expires, not three weeks after delivery. Doing that by hand across hundreds of customers does not happen.

## How recovery works

Recovering F&I revenue is a workflow problem, not a sales skills problem. The pieces are:

**Triggers from your own data.** Warranty end dates, loan age, lease end dates, and declined-product flags are already recorded. A [CRM and lead system](/services/crm-lead-systems) connected to the DMS can watch for them automatically.

**One message, at the right moment.** When a trigger fires, the customer gets a short, plain text or email about the one product that applies: coverage ending next month, a payment that could be lower, a lease decision coming up. No menu, no pitch for everything at once.

**A fast handoff to a person.** Replies route to the F&I manager or a BDC agent within minutes, with the customer's deal history attached. The conversation starts informed, not cold.

**Honest tracking.** Count products sold from the follow-up separately from products sold in the office, so the program is measured on its own numbers.

## What to measure

Keep the scorecard simple:

- Customers contacted per trigger type
- Reply rate and appointment rate
- Products sold and F&I gross from follow-up, by product
- Refinance and lease-return deals that originated from a trigger

Divide F&I gross recovered by deliveries in the same period and you get a number every dealer principal understands: recovered F&I dollars per 100 deliveries.

## Compliance comes first

Post-sale outreach touches two rule sets. Texting requires the customer's prior consent and a working opt-out, under the TCPA. Product language has to be accurate: a vehicle service contract is a service contract, not a warranty, and the message should say so. Build both into the workflow from the start so every message that goes out is compliant by default.

## Start with one trigger

Dealerships that try to recover everything at once usually recover nothing. Pick the single highest-value trigger, usually expiring factory coverage against a declined service contract, run it for 90 days, and measure. Then add the next one.
