A new HVAC system costs about what a used car does, lasts about as long, and is financed by most of the customers who buy one. Yet the way most HVAC companies sell it looks nothing like a dealership's finance office. The price is a lump sum, financing is mentioned if the customer asks, and nothing is offered alongside the install. The F&I playbook that dealerships have refined for decades applies directly, and it changes the economics of every install.

## Present the payment, not the price

Dealers learned long ago that customers decide on the monthly figure. An HVAC quote that says "$X installed" invites a comparison with the cheapest competitor. A quote that says "$X installed, or about $Y per month" invites a decision. Both numbers should be on every proposal, with the financing terms stated plainly.

The payment also reframes the good-better-best choice. The difference between a standard and a high-efficiency system might be a large sum up front and a small amount per month. Customers upgrade when they see it that way.

## The protection products

F&I sells coverage alongside the vehicle. The HVAC equivalents:

- **Extended labor coverage or a service agreement.** The manufacturer's parts warranty is the factory warranty; labor coverage is the service contract. Use accurate terms for each.
- **Maintenance plans.** Prepaid maintenance, with seasonal tune-ups and priority service. The HVAC version of prepaid maintenance.
- **Indoor air quality add-ons.** Filtration, humidity control, UV. Offered at install, when the system is open and the labor is already on site.
- **Surge and equipment protection.** Where offered, the analogue of GAP: a small product that matters when something goes wrong.

Present them once at the proposal, in plain language, and record what was declined. The declines are the input for follow-up, exactly as in F&I.

## Follow up on declines at the right moment

A customer who declined the maintenance plan at install may say yes at the first seasonal reminder. A customer who declined labor coverage may say yes a month before the labor warranty ends. The lifecycle triggers that a dealership runs on in-service dates run on install dates here:

- Install plus 30 days: satisfaction check and maintenance plan offer.
- First season change: tune-up booking, plan offer for customers without one.
- Labor warranty end minus 60 days: coverage offer.
- Year three onward: repair-versus-replace conversations as estimates appear.

A [CRM connected to the field service platform](/services/crm-lead-systems) fires these from the install record. Replies book through the same conversation, by text or through an [AI receptionist](/services/ai-voice-receptionist) that can see the technician schedule.

## Financing as a lead source

Financing is not only a closing tool. It is a reason to reach customers earlier:

- **Repair-versus-replace.** A customer facing a large repair on an old system can see a new-system payment beside the repair cost. This is the declined-repair-as-financing-lead logic from the dealership world.
- **Pre-qualification.** A soft pre-qualification link in reactivation messages lets customers see what they qualify for before a visit, which removes the biggest reason people delay a replacement.
- **Seasonal offers to the install base.** Promotional financing on replacements, sent to customers whose systems are past a certain age.

## What to measure

- Share of proposals presented with a monthly payment
- Financing attach rate and average financed amount
- Protection products sold at install and from follow-up
- Maintenance plan penetration across the install base
- Replacements sold from repair-versus-replace conversations

## Compliance

Financing offers must reflect the lender's actual terms and approval process. Products must be described accurately: a manufacturer's warranty is a warranty; a labor plan or service agreement is a contract. Texts need consent and an opt-out. Promotional financing terms, including any deferred-interest conditions, must be disclosed in full.
