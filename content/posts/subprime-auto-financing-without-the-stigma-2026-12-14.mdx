The language around subprime auto financing is mostly shame-based. "Bad credit? No problem!" "Guaranteed approval!" "Everyone drives!" It attracts attention and repels trust. The customers it targets are not careless. They are people with a thin file, a past event, or a few hard years, who need a reliable car to keep a job. A dealership or lender that talks to them like adults, with clear terms and a path forward, earns a customer who comes back when their credit is prime.

## Who the subprime customer is

Drop the stereotype and look at the file:

- A young buyer with no credit history yet.
- A recent immigrant with income but no file.
- Someone recovering from a medical bill, a divorce, or a layoff.
- A self-employed person whose income does not fit the standard form.
- A customer who repaired a past default and has been paying everything on time for two years.

What they share is a need for transportation and a decent chance of paying. What they do not share is a desire to be shouted at.

## Why "guaranteed approval" marketing fails

Loud subprime advertising has three problems:

1. **It is often untrue.** Nothing is guaranteed, and the customer learns that in the finance office.
2. **It attracts the wrong applications.** The customers who respond to "no credit check" are not the ones a lender wants.
3. **It marks the dealership.** Prime customers avoid a store that looks like it only sells to desperate people.

The alternative is not to stop marketing to subprime customers. It is to market the way a credit union would: plainly.

## Plain-language marketing that works

**Lead with the process, not the promise.** "We work with several lenders who finance customers with limited or damaged credit. Here is what we need from you and what to expect." That is more reassuring than any exclamation mark.

**Pre-qualify softly.** A soft-pull pre-qualification, clearly explained as not affecting the credit score, lets the customer see realistic numbers before they come in. It also saves the finance office from a wasted afternoon.

**Show the path.** "Pay on time for 12 months and we'll review your rate." A credit-rebuilding offer turns a one-time deal into a relationship.

**Keep payment claims honest.** "Payments from X" is only acceptable if a real customer can get X. Price the example from an actual approval.

## Follow-up that respects the customer

Subprime customers are used to being ignored once the deal funds. Flip that:

- **Welcome and payment setup.** A message after funding confirming the first due date and how to pay. Missed first payments are often confusion, not refusal.
- **Reminders before due dates.** A short reminder a few days before each payment. This is delinquency prevention, and it protects the customer's rebuild as much as the lender's portfolio.
- **The 12-month review.** When the customer has paid on time for a year, reach out: "You've built a strong payment history. You may qualify for a better rate. Want us to check?" This is the "lower your payment" message, and it is the moment the subprime customer becomes a prime customer with you instead of with someone else.

## Where automation helps and where it should not

Automation should handle timing: pre-qualification links, reminders, anniversary reviews, and reply routing. It should not handle the credit decision, the explanation of terms, or a customer in financial difficulty. Those go to a person, every time, and the workflow should be built to hand them over.

## What to measure

- Pre-qualification completion rate
- Approval rate and average time to funding
- First-payment default rate (a quality measure for the welcome process)
- On-time payment rate at 6 and 12 months
- Share of subprime customers refinanced or repeat-purchased at a better tier

The last number tells you whether the program builds customers or just books loans.

## Compliance

Subprime lending is heavily regulated. Advertising claims must be accurate and substantiated. Soft pre-qualification must be described correctly. Texting needs consent and an opt-out, and collection-related messages have their own rules under the FDCPA where it applies. Build the messages with counsel's review, then automate them exactly as approved.
