A car dealership's most valuable asset is not the showroom. It is the service drive: a stream of known customers with known vehicles, each one due for something on a date the dealer can predict. An HVAC company has exactly the same asset and usually treats it as a filing cabinet. Every system you have ever installed is a customer with known equipment, a known install date, and a known future: filters, tune-ups, repairs, and eventually a replacement.

That install base is where most of your future revenue already lives.

## What the install base knows

For every installed system, your records hold:

- **Equipment type, brand, and model.** Which tells you expected lifespan and common failure points.
- **Install date.** Which tells you when maintenance is due, when the warranty ends, and roughly when replacement conversations start.
- **Service history.** Which tells you whether the customer maintains the system, and which repairs have already been done.
- **Contact details and consent.** Which tells you how you are allowed to reach them.

A dealership uses the same four facts to fill its service lane. Most HVAC companies use them only when the customer calls.

## The lifecycle, dated

Map the install base onto a timeline and the revenue events are obvious:

1. **Year 1.** Filter reminders and a first-year check. Low value per customer, high value for retention.
2. **Years 1 to 10.** Seasonal tune-ups, spring and fall. This is where a maintenance agreement should be offered, because a customer on a plan calls you first.
3. **Warranty expiry.** Parts warranties end on a known date. Just before that date is the right time to offer a service agreement or a repair inspection.
4. **Years 10 and up.** Repair costs rise. Each repair estimate is a chance to discuss replacement and financing, the same way a declined car repair becomes a sales conversation at a dealership.
5. **Replacement.** The system you installed is replaced by you, or by whoever marketed to the customer last.

Every event has a date. Dates can be automated.

## Turning records into revenue

The mechanics are the same ones that work for [database reactivation](/services/database-reactivation) in any service business:

**Segment by lifecycle stage.** Split the install base by system age and last service date. A 12-year-old system with no service in two years is a different conversation from a 2-year-old system on a plan.

**One message per trigger.** A short text or email tied to the event: tune-up season, warranty ending, a system reaching the age where repair costs climb. Not a newsletter.

**Book from the reply.** When the customer replies, the booking should happen in that conversation, by text or by an [AI receptionist](/services/ai-voice-receptionist) that can see your calendar. Replies that wait a day go cold.

**Offer the plan at the right moment.** The maintenance agreement sells best right after a service visit or just before a warranty ends, not in a cold email.

## Why this beats buying leads

New leads from search ads or lead-generation sites are expensive, shared with competitors, and unknown to you. Install base customers:

- cost nothing to reach beyond the message
- already chose you once
- have equipment you understand
- can be contacted at the exact moment the need exists

A dealership would never let a service customer leave without the next appointment booked. An HVAC company that treats its install base the same way builds a recurring revenue floor under the business.

## What to measure

- Install base contacted per month, by trigger
- Reply and booking rates
- Maintenance agreements sold
- Repairs and replacements booked from triggered outreach
- Share of replacements that come from your own install base

That last number is the one to watch. If customers you installed are replacing with someone else, the install base is leaking.

## Compliance

Texting customers requires their consent and a clear opt-out. Keep the consent record in the CRM, honor opt-outs instantly, and send only when there is a real reason tied to their equipment.
