Dealerships measure gross per unit at the moment of sale and then stop. The vehicle, the F&I products, and the trade are counted; everything that happens in the years after delivery is counted somewhere else, if at all. Revenue recovered per 100 deliveries is a way of putting the after-delivery value on the same scale as the sale, so the dealer can see what each delivery is really worth and how much of it the store is capturing.

## The definition

For a cohort of 100 vehicles delivered in a given month, total the revenue that later came from those same customers through tracked follow-up:

- Service and parts revenue from booked service visits (not walk-ins)
- F&I products sold after delivery: service contracts, GAP, maintenance, tire and wheel
- Refinance, buyout, or lease-renewal deals
- Trade-ins and replacement vehicle sales originated by a trigger
- Service-drive acquisitions from those customers

Divide by the cohort size and multiply by 100. The result is revenue recovered per 100 deliveries, measured at 12, 24, and 36 months.

## Why per 100 deliveries

- **It is comparable across stores.** A 300-unit store and a 100-unit store can be compared directly.
- **It separates recovery from volume.** Sales volume can rise while recovery falls, and the metric shows it.
- **It is a budget number.** If the store knows what 100 deliveries recover, it knows what a recovery program is worth before buying one.
- **It compounds visibly.** Each monthly cohort adds a line, and the chart shows whether the store is getting better at keeping customers.

## Calculating it honestly

The counting rules from earlier in this series apply:

1. **Attribution requires a tracked path.** The customer booked, replied, or arrived through a follow-up conversation. Walk-ins and unprompted visits are retention, which is a separate and also useful number, but not recovery.
2. **Revenue comes from the DMS.** Repair order totals, F&I product gross, deal gross. Not estimates from the automation platform.
3. **Cohorts are fixed.** A customer belongs to the month they were delivered. Their later purchases count toward that cohort.
4. **Costs are tracked beside it.** Messages, platform fees, and staff time, so net recovery is visible.

A [CRM connected to the DMS](/services/crm-lead-systems) can produce the cohort table as a standing report once the attribution rules are in the data.

## What drives the number

Each trigger in this series feeds it:

- Service reminders and the first-service booking drive the 12-month service line.
- Warranty-end and declined-product follow-up drive post-sale F&I.
- Equity and rate triggers drive refinance and trade.
- Lease maturity drives renewals.
- Declined repairs and service-drive acquisition drive replacement sales.

A store that runs none of them recovers whatever customers bring on their own. A store that runs all of them on one engine recovers a multiple of that.

## Raising it

Start from the cohort table and find the smallest line. If 12-month service revenue is low, fix contact rate and first-service booking. If post-sale F&I is near zero, the declines are not being recorded or followed. If refinance is empty, the equity trigger does not exist yet. Each gap maps to a specific program, and the metric tells you which one to build next.

## Benchmarking

There is no universal benchmark worth quoting, because product mix, service pricing, and customer base vary too much between stores. The useful benchmark is your own: this quarter's cohorts against last year's. A rising line is the goal.

## What to report

- Revenue recovered per 100 deliveries at 12, 24, and 36 months, by cohort month
- The same number split by source line
- Net of program cost
- Retention (unprompted return) beside it, for context

Put this on the dealer principal's page, under revenue. It is the number that shows whether the store is building a customer base or just selling cars.
