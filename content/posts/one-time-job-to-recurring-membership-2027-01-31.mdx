A home service business that completes a repair and sends an invoice has earned one job. The same business that completes the repair and signs the customer up for a maintenance membership has earned a customer: seasonal visits, priority service, a discount on repairs, and the next replacement. The difference is not the work. It is whether the membership was offered, when, and what happened afterwards.

## Why memberships matter

- **Revenue floor.** Monthly or annual fees arrive whether or not the phone rings.
- **First call.** Members call the business they pay, not the first search result.
- **Scheduled visits.** Tune-ups fill the slow months and keep technicians in front of the equipment that will need replacing.
- **Higher lifetime value.** Members buy more repairs, more upgrades, and the eventual replacement from the company they already pay.
- **Business value.** A recurring revenue base is worth more to a buyer than the same revenue earned job by job.

## The moments to offer it

Memberships sell at specific moments, not in a newsletter:

1. **At job completion.** The technician has just fixed the problem; trust is highest. "A plan would have covered the diagnostic today and gets you two tune-ups a year. Want me to add it?"
2. **With the install proposal.** A new system plus a plan at a combined monthly figure. The F&I playbook applies.
3. **At the seasonal reminder.** A customer booking a tune-up is a customer who values maintenance. Offer the plan that includes it.
4. **After a declined repair.** A customer who declines a large repair may still take a plan that spreads the maintenance cost and includes a discount.
5. **At the first anniversary of a non-member's install.** The warranty period is a natural review point.

Each moment is an event in the CRM or field service platform, and each can fire an offer automatically.

## Automating the follow-up

The technician's offer at the job is the start. The system handles the rest:

- **Same-day text** to non-members after a job: a one-line plan offer with a reply option.
- **Seasonal triggers** for non-members, timed to the equipment and the season.
- **Renewal reminders** for members, 30 days before expiry, with a one-reply renewal.
- **Lapsed-member recovery**: a message 60 days after a plan lapses, with the first-visit booking offered as the way back in.
- **Replies that book.** An [AI receptionist](/services/ai-voice-receptionist) or text flow confirms the plan, takes the payment method, and books the first visit in one conversation.

A [CRM built for service businesses](/services/crm-lead-systems) runs all of it from the job and equipment records.

## Designing the plan

Keep it simple enough to explain in one sentence:

- Two visits a year (or one for simpler equipment)
- Priority scheduling
- A repair discount
- No diagnostic fee for members
- A clear monthly or annual price

Complicated tiers slow the sale. One good plan, with an optional premium level for customers who ask, is enough.

## What to measure

- Membership offer rate at job completion (did the technician ask?)
- Conversion at each offer moment
- Members as a share of the active customer base
- Renewal rate and lapse recovery rate
- Revenue per member per year versus revenue per non-member

The offer rate is the first thing to fix. Memberships that are not offered do not sell.

## Compliance

Recurring billing requires clear terms, consent to the charge, and an easy cancellation path. Texts need consent and an opt-out. State the plan's price and what it includes accurately in every message.
